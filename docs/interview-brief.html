Engineering brief · Order-to-Cash

CashMatch

An AI cash application agent: match incoming B2B bank payments to the open invoices they settle, apply cash when confident, review when not.

Live · 13.235.237.109 All 8 phases complete Python 3.13 · FastAPI · SQLAlchemy 2 PostgreSQL 16 Gemini (optional) 2,000 invoices · 800 payments 491 tests passing

01The pitch

A distributor's customer wires ₹4,18,500. Nothing in the bank statement line says which invoices it settles, and the note that explains it arrived two days earlier, by email, with no reference tying the two together.

What the bank gives you
06-Feb-2026 NEFT  A.B.C. TRDRS PVT PMT REF 42/43 DED DMG ₹4,18,500.00
What it actually means
INV-00042 due 09-Feb ₹3,00,000.00 applied in full
INV-00043 due 14-Feb ₹1,30,500.00 −₹12,000.00 damage
Cash applied ₹4,18,500.00 balances ✓

One payment, two invoices, one deduction. An AR analyst resolves this by hand several hundred times a day. CashMatch does it automatically and explains every decision it makes.

What the system has to get right

A single bank credit might settle one invoice, bundle a dozen, arrive short because the customer is claiming damaged stock, quote a mangled reference, or belong to no invoice at all. The remittance advice that disambiguates it is a free-text email or a PDF, on a separate channel, often late.

Two numbers decide whether the system is worth deploying.

81.9% Auto-match rate
100% Match precision
13.0% Review rate
5.1% Unapplied rate

Measured over 800 payments worth ₹61.1 crore, against a ground-truth file the matcher cannot read. Zero payments were auto-applied to the wrong invoice. Full breakdown in Results.

02Domain primer

Order-to-Cash is everything between a customer placing an order and the money landing, reconciled, in your ledger. Cash application is the last step: deciding which invoices a received payment settles, and posting it. These are the terms the code uses.

TermWhat it meansWorked example
Open item An invoice with money still outstanding. The search space the matcher works against. INV-00042, ₹3,00,000 billed, ₹3,00,000 still open.
Remittance advice The customer's note saying what a payment covers. Travels on a different channel from the money — email, PDF, portal — and frequently carries no bank reference. “Pls find payment for 42 & 43, dmg claim adjusted.”
Short-pay The customer deliberately pays less than billed. Not late payment and not bad debt — they are asserting a claim and expect you to accept it. ₹1,30,500 invoice settled with ₹1,18,500.
Deduction The withheld amount, plus a reason code. Routing the claim to the right team (logistics, trade marketing, pricing) is a whole downstream workflow. ₹12,000, reason damage.
Unapplied cash Money received that could not be matched. It is not lost — it sits on the customer's account, making them look like they owe less than they do. ₹7,500 from a payer with no open invoices.
Partial payment The customer pays part of an invoice with the rest still to come. The invoice becomes partially_paid and stays in the open-item pool. ₹1,00,000 against a ₹3,00,000 invoice.
DSO Days Sales Outstanding — average days to collect. Unapplied cash inflates it artificially, which is why finance leadership cares about this system. A 2-day cut in DSO on ₹50 crore of receivables frees real working capital.
Dunning Automated chasing of overdue invoices. If cash was mis-applied, dunning chases a customer for an invoice they already paid. The most visible failure mode of a wrong auto-match.

Why it is genuinely hard

  • The payer name is not the customer name. Banks relay whatever the remitting bank sent: A.B.C. TRDRS PVT, M/s ABC Traders, ABC TRADERS-MUMBAI. All one customer.
  • The reference field is a free-text dumping ground. Sometimes invoice numbers, sometimes the customer's internal payment ID, sometimes nothing.
  • Amounts rarely tie out exactly. Deductions, TDS withholding, bank charges and rounding all move the number.
  • Bundled payments are combinatorial. One credit against 40 open invoices has a very large number of candidate subsets, and several may sum to the same total.

03Cost asymmetry

The single most important idea in the project, and the one worth leading with in an interview: the two ways this system can fail do not cost the same.

FailureWhat happensCost to unwind
Sent to review
when it could have been auto		 An analyst opens the item, agrees with the suggestion, clicks approve. Roughly two minutes of analyst time. Bounded, visible, and it still produces the correct ledger entry.
Auto-applied
to the wrong invoice		 An invoice is marked paid that is not. A different invoice stays open. The customer's balance is wrong in two places at once. A dunning letter goes to a customer who paid. Someone disputes it. A reversal entry is raised, approved, posted, and reconciled. Hours of work across two teams, plus the relationship damage.

Consequence for the design: precision is a hard constraint and automation rate is the thing being optimised underneath it. The right question is never “how high can we push the auto-match rate?” but “what is the highest auto-match rate we can reach while holding precision above the bar finance will accept?”

That is why Phase 6 reports a threshold sweep rather than a single accuracy number. Moving the auto-apply threshold trades one failure mode for the other, and the business — not the engineer — should choose where on that curve to sit.

It is also why every decision carries a stored explanation. A reviewer who cannot see why the system suggested something has to redo the work from scratch, which erases the saving the review queue was supposed to deliver.

04Architecture

Two inputs arrive independently and have to be fused: the bank statement carries the money, the remittance advice carries the intent. Everything downstream is a cascade of progressively weaker signals, each one explaining itself as it fires.

The matching cascade, in order

Each step is tried in turn and stops when it produces a confident candidate set. The order is deliberate: cheapest and most certain first.

#StrategyWhat it doesCost
1 reference_exact A normalised invoice reference found in the narration or the extracted remittance hits an open item directly. Index lookup
2 amount_exact Exactly one of the customer's open invoices equals the payment to the paise. Indexed scan
3 subset_sum Find a set of open invoices summing to the payment within tolerance — the bundled-payment case. Pruned search
4 short_pay Find a set whose total exceeds the payment by a plausible deduction, and classify the gap. Pruned search

On subset-sum complexity. Subset-sum is NP-complete in general, so the engineering is all in the pruning. Restricting to one customer's open items cuts the candidate pool from every invoice in the business to typically 10–60; a date window around the payment trims it further; and capping the combination size at k bounds the work at C(n, k) rather than 2ⁿ. With n = 40 and k = 4 that is about 102,000 combinations — a few milliseconds — against 1.1 × 10¹² for the unpruned power set.

The honest trade-off: capping at k means a genuine 7-invoice bundle will not be found by this strategy. That is the right failure — it lands in the review queue rather than producing a wrong auto-match.

05Data model

Eight tables. The shape of the schema encodes the shape of the business problem, and two of the decisions below are the ones worth defending in an interview: the nullable remittance link, and the absence of any ground-truth column.

explanation is a first-class

06The dataset

A fictional Indian FMCG distributor's receivables book, generated from a seed and shipped with an answer key. Real AR data cannot go in a portfolio project, and hand-built fixtures would be far too tidy to measure anything — so the dataset is built to contain, in known proportions, every failure mode the matcher has to survive.

Everything about its shape lives in one YAML file. That is the knob: raise missing_reference to 40, re-run the generator, watch the auto-match rate fall. Keeping it out of code makes that a thirty-second experiment instead of a code change.

backend/config/scenarios.yml

scenarios:
  exact_single:        34   # clean reference, exact amount
  bundled_multi:       18   # one credit, several invoices - needs subset-sum
  short_pay_deduction: 14   # pays less, says why
  partial_payment:     10   # part now, rest later
  missing_reference:   10   # no usable reference at all
  typo_reference:       8   # "inv 42" for "INV-00042"
  payer_name_variant:   4   # spelling not on file - needs fuzzy matching
  no_matching_invoice:  2   # should end as unapplied cash

Strictly validated: the mix must sum to exactly 100, every key must name a known scenario, and a typo in a key is an error rather than a setting that silently does nothing.

A forwarded email thread with quote markers, an amount in Indian digit grouping with a trailing slash, and a claim explained in prose. Four other template families appear across the corpus, plus 74 PDFs — because an extractor that only handles one house style will visibly underperform, and Phase 6 should be able to see that in the numbers.

Why the answer key cannot be cheated

Ground truth is written to data/ground_truth/ground_truth.json and never loaded into the database the matcher queries. The separation is physical, not a convention, and three things hold it in place.

The schema carries no scenario or label column on bank_transactions, and a test fails the build if one containing scenario, truth or expected ever appears. The database is handed over in its pre-matching state — every invoice fully open, every payment unmatched. And Phase 6's evaluator is the only component that opens the file, after matching has already finished.

Reproducibility, and why it earns its keep

One seeded Random drives every choice, so the same config always produces byte-identical data — verified by a test that generates twice and diffs the answer key. The config itself is fingerprinted into the output (sha256:787363436b79d3af), so an accuracy result can always be traced back to the exact scenario mix it was measured against.

Without that, "we improved from 71% to 78%" is an unfalsifiable claim. With it, either the comparison is real or the digests disagree and you know not to make it.

Honest limitations of this data

  • One payment per invoice. Exclusive consumption keeps the answer key unambiguous, at the cost of ruling out the genuine two-instalment case.
  • Unmatchable payments come from strangers. They carry a payer name belonging to no customer. The subtler real case — a known customer overpaying, or paying an invoice not yet raised — is not modelled.
  • The messiness is messiness I thought of. This is the limitation that actually matters. Real bank files contain failure modes nobody anticipates, and the honest expectation is that measured accuracy drops on first contact with real data.

07The cascade

Six steps per bank credit, ordered by decreasing certainty and increasing cost. The cascade stops at the first step that produces candidates — running a combinatorial search behind an already-certain reference hit is pure waste.

Every step that ran is still recorded, firing or not. A reviewer needs to know the matcher looked for a reference and found none, not merely that it produced nothing.

Measured on the 800-payment dataset

97.2% Produced candidates
17 Ambiguous
22 No candidates
0.4s Whole run

Candidate rate is not the auto-match rate. Some of those 778 are ambiguous and some will be wrong. Scoring and thresholding are Phase 5; measuring correctness against the answer key is Phase 6. Quoting 97.2% as an auto-match rate would be exactly the kind of overclaim an interviewer is listening for.

Why subset-sum does not explode

Subset-sum is NP-complete, so every bit of the engineering is in the pruning. Three prunes run before the search starts: scope to the identified customer, trim to a date window around the payment, and cap the combination size at k.

With n = 45 candidate invoices, k = 5Combinations
Unpruned power set, 2n 35,184,372,088,832
Admitted by the size cap, Σ C(45, i ≤ 5) 1,385,979
Actually visited, after the arithmetic prunes tens to hundreds
Across the entire 800-payment run 681,620

The size cap turns O(2ⁿ) into O(nk / k!) — polynomial in n for fixed k. The two arithmetic prunes then cut most of what remains, and they work because every amount is positive: abandon a branch once the running total overshoots, and abandon the whole tail once the running total plus everything still available falls short. Sorting candidates descending makes both fire early.

Integer paise is what makes this answerable at all. “Does this set total exactly the payment?” is an equality test on integers; over floats it would be an epsilon comparison, and a subset-sum over epsilon comparisons is not a well-posed question.

The cost of the cap, stated plainly. A genuine seven-invoice bundle will not be found at k = 5. That is the correct failure: it goes to the review queue rather than producing a confident wrong answer. Every limit in this engine — the size cap, the search budget, the ambiguity margin — is tuned to fail towards review rather than towards a guess.

Two bugs the tests caught

Worth having ready: interviewers ask what went wrong, and “nothing” is never a good answer. Both of these were found by assertions, not by reading the code.

BugWhat happened, and the fix
Deduction plausibility
measured against the
wrong denominator		 A payment resolved all three of its invoices correctly and the gap was exactly the real claim — ₹4,900 on a ₹56,865 invoice, a routine 8.6% damage adjustment. But plausibility was being measured against the three-invoice remittance total of ₹10.9 lakh, where the same claim is 0.45% and fell below the floor. The reading was rejected and the payment fell through to a combinatorial search that produced three wrong ambiguous readings. A deduction is raised against one invoice, so it must be judged against that invoice. Fixing the denominator cut ambiguous outcomes from 30 to 17.
The amount tolerance
was inventing money		 Five invoices totalled 31 paise more than the payment — inside the ₹1 tolerance, so the set matched and every invoice was allocated in full. That applies 31 paise of cash the bank never sent, and the ledger would not balance. The tolerance exists so rounding does not cost a match, not so the ledger can invent money. The difference is now written off against the smallest invoice, keeping both invariants true: applied cash ties to the credit received, and each line still clears its invoice.

The second one is the more interesting story. It was caught by a property test asserting that no candidate ever allocates more than arrived — the kind of invariant that is obvious once written down and invisible until then. It would never have shown up in a demo; it would have shown up in a reconciliation six months into production.

What the engine refuses to do

SituationResponse
Two customers score 94.4 and 94.4 Refuse. An ambiguity margin requires the best fuzzy match to beat the runner-up by a configured gap. Two names a point apart is a coin flip, and a coin flip that posts money against the wrong account is the failure this project exists to avoid.
Payer name says one customer,
the reference says another		 Refuse, and record both readings. Picking one would be guessing with high apparent confidence.
Several invoice sets
tie on amount		 Return all of them and flag the outcome ambiguous. The reviewer sees the real alternatives rather than an arbitrary pick.
A reference like INV-00042-A Do not resolve it to INV-00042. A revision suffix makes it a different document, and quietly matching it would be confident and wrong.
The search budget runs out Stop, and say so in the diagnostics. A silent partial search that reports "no match" is worse than one that admits it gave up.

Where Phase 3 deliberately stops

The engine writes nothing. It answers “which invoices could this payment be settling, and by what reasoning?” and hands over a signal trail. It does not answer “should we post this automatically?”

Keeping those apart means the thresholds can be re-tuned in Phase 5 without re-running the search, and that the same candidate set can be scored differently for a conservative client and an aggressive one. It also means a test can assert, flatly, that matching changes no row in the database — which it does.

08Extraction

The LLM proposes structure. It never decides anything, and nothing it says is believed without the arithmetic agreeing.

That sentence is the whole design. Everything below is the machinery that makes it true, and the reason a language model is safe to point at a ledger at all.

The five layers, and what each one is for

LayerWhat it prevents
Amounts typed as
strings in the schema		 A model asked for a number emits 418500.1 and json.loads hands back a float. By the time that float exists the precision damage is done. Strings force the conversion through rupees_to_paise(), which refuses anything it cannot represent exactly. This is rule 1 of the project expressed as a type, not a code review comment.
Strict Pydantic
validation		 Malformed structure never reaches the matcher. A line claiming to overpay its own invoice is dropped; a reference that cannot be normalised is dropped; the rest of the document survives.
One retry, with the
error fed back		 A blind second call usually reproduces the first mistake. Returning the validator's complaint to the model is what makes the retry worth paying for. One retry, not three — past that the rules are cheaper and more predictable.
Rule-based fallback Total dependence on an external service. It handles the common shapes outright, needs no key and no network, and the method used is recorded per row so Phase 5 can weight an LLM result differently from a regex one.
Arithmetic cross-check
against the real credit		 The one that matters. A hallucinated figure almost never balances against a real bank payment. An extraction that does not tie is kept but flagged — its invoice references are often still correct — and the matcher re-verifies the arithmetic independently before proposing anything.

That is the resilience the design promises, demonstrated against a real outage rather than a mock of one.

09Confidence

Five signals, a weighted sum, three penalties, two thresholds. The arithmetic is simple on purpose.

A controller can be walked through any number this produces in about a minute, and a client who wants fewer errors moves one threshold and sees the trade immediately. A gradient-boosted model over the same signals would almost certainly score better once there is labelled review history — and the breakdown stored on every decision is exactly the training set it would need.

SignalWeightWhat it asks
reference_match 0.34 Did the customer actually name these invoices — in the narration or in their own advice? By far the strongest evidence available.
amount_match 0.26 How cleanly does the money land? An exact tie scores full; a subset found by search scores 0.80 because several sets can tie; an inferred deduction scores 0.55 because it is a guess about intent.
customer_identity 0.18 Exact name and known alias both score 1.0 — an alias is an exact lookup against a spelling a human already confirmed. A fuzzy hit carries its own similarity score.
remittance_agreement 0.14 Does the customer's own advice corroborate this reading, and did its arithmetic tie against the credit?
date_proximity 0.08 Is the money arriving when these invoices would plausibly settle? The weakest signal by design — a chronic late payer is still paying their own invoices, so this breaks ties and decides nothing.

Penalties, applied as multipliers

Multiplicative rather than subtractive on purpose: an ambiguity should scale down whatever confidence the evidence produced, not shave a fixed amount off it. Two invoice sets tying on amount is just as damning for a strong match as a weak one.

PenaltyMultiplierWhen, and why that size
ambiguous ×0.55 Several readings tie and nothing separates them. Sized so it sinks even a flawless match below the auto threshold on its own — a test asserts that. Picking one would be a coin flip posted with high confidence.
unreconciled_advice ×0.92 The advice guiding the match did not reconcile against the credit. Mild, because its invoice references are usually still right.
residual_cash ×0.85 Not every paise could be placed. A match that cannot account for the whole credit should not post itself.

The bug that was worth 65 points of auto-match rate

First run of the scorer: 22.6% auto-applied, with 477 of 800 payments piled into the 0.80–0.89 band — directly under the 0.90 threshold. The obvious move is to drop the threshold to 0.85.

That would have been wrong, and would have hidden the actual defect. Here is the signal breakdown of the single strongest match in the whole run:

UTR2026012500682   base = 0.8600

  reference_match        value 1.00   weight 0.34   ->  0.3400
  amount_match           value 1.00   weight 0.26   ->  0.2600
  customer_identity      value 1.00   weight 0.18   ->  0.1800
  remittance_agreement   value 0.00   weight 0.14   ->  0.0000   <-- no advice exists
  date_proximity         value 1.00   weight 0.08   ->  0.0800
                                                       ------
                                                       0.8600

Exact reference, exact amount, exact payer name, paid on time. There is nothing more this match could have done — and it could never reach 0.90, because the 0.14 advice weight sat in the denominator for the three quarters of customers who simply never send an advice document. The signal was behaving as a penalty for something that is not a fault.

The fix: score against the evidence that exists, not the evidence that could exist. Each signal now declares whether it is applicable, and the weighted sum divides by the applicable weight only. Confidence then means "how much of the available evidence supports this" — the only thing it can honestly mean.

Note what stays applicable: a missing reference still scores zero rather than being excluded, because the absence of a reference genuinely weakens a match. And excluding the advice weight makes that cost larger: with no advice, the reference now carries 0.34/0.86 of the decision instead of 0.34/1.00.

Auto-match rate: 22.6% → 87.2%. The threshold never moved.

Recording a decision is not posting the cash

Two steps, deliberately separate. apply writes a match_results row with its allocations and the full explanation, and changes no invoice balance. apply --post additionally reduces open amounts and marks transactions.

That split is not ceremony. A real first deployment runs in suggest-only mode for weeks while the client watches the precision number, and an engine that can only run in post mode cannot be deployed that way at all.

Posting, verified end to endResult
Open receivables before → after ₹96.5 cr → ₹43.2 cr
Invoices moved to paid / partially paid 1,043 / 79
Transactions marked matched698

Invoices driven negative0
Re-running --post 0 invoices touched

Idempotency comes from the transaction's own status, which is what that column has been there for since Phase 1. Posting the same payment twice would clear its invoices twice and corrupt the customer's balance, so a payment that is no longer unmatched is never posted again.

One more guard worth naming: re-running the engine replaces its own previous verdict, but never one a human has touched. Overwriting an analyst's judgement with a fresh machine guess would be the worst behaviour available here.

    {"name": "reference_match",   "fired": true,  "applicable": true,

applicable_weight and excluded_signals are there so the contributions visibly add up. Without them a reviewer cannot check the arithmetic by hand, and an explanation you cannot verify is not an explanation.

10The product

Twelve REST endpoints and a React review queue whose only job is to make a review decision take two minutes instead of twenty.

This is the part a client actually touches. Everything before it is machinery; this is where an AR analyst spends their morning, and the measure of it is whether they can act on an item without reconstructing the engine's reasoning first.

Money never crosses the wire as a number

{
  "statement_ref": "UTR2026080400580",
  "amount": { "paise": 74635976, "display": "₹7,46,359.76" },
  "confidence": 0.6,
  "decision": "needs_review"
}

A JSON number for money invites a float on the other side, and a balance that is one paise wrong is impossible to explain to a customer. Every amount is an integer with a formatted string beside it: the integer is authoritative, the string is for display, and the UI never does arithmetic on either.

A test walks every API payload recursively and asserts that every money-shaped object has an int in it. Rule 1 of the project, enforced at the boundary rather than hoped for.

What a reviewer sees

✕ reference_match       0.00 × 0.34 = 0.000   No invoice reference anywhere;
                                                the match rests on amount and payer.
✓ amount_match          1.00 × 0.26 = 0.260   Found by amount_exact, settling as exact.
✓ customer_identity     1.00 × 0.18 = 0.180   Payer matched a spelling already on file.
– remittance_agreement         not applicable  No advice is linked to this payment, so
                                                there is nothing to corroborate against.

base 0.600 ÷ applicable weight 0.86 → confidence 60.0% · excluded: remittance_agreement

The arithmetic is shown because an explanation you cannot check by hand is not an explanation. 0.260 + 0.180 + 0.076 = 0.516, divided by 0.86, is 60.0% — a reviewer can verify that in their head and decide whether they agree with the weighting, not just with the answer.

Three signal states, not two. Fired, did not fire, and not applicable. That distinction is the one the Phase 5 bug turned on, and showing it means a reviewer never wonders whether the matcher looked at something or simply found nothing there.

Three guards on anything that moves money

GuardWhy it has to exist
An item cannot be
reviewed twice		 Two analysts working the same queue must not both post the same cash. The second attempt gets a 422 naming who already handled it and when — not a bare error, because the next thing that person does depends on knowing that.
A reassignment cannot
over-apply		 Two separate checks: not more than the invoice still has open, and not more than the bank actually sent. People make arithmetic mistakes and a UI can be used wrongly. Each refusal quotes the real figures rather than saying "invalid".
A human's decision is
never overwritten		 Re-running the engine replaces its own previous verdict, but any result with reviewed_at set is left alone. Replacing an analyst's judgement with a fresh machine guess is the worst behaviour available here.

The feedback loop, finally closed

When an analyst approves a match whose payer spelling the matcher could not resolve, that spelling is written to the alias table as source = 'learned'. The next payment from the same spelling resolves by index lookup instead of fuzzy matching, and never reaches the queue at all.

That is the loop the customer_aliases table was designed for in Phase 1, drawn as the dashed path in the architecture diagram, and only now actually wired up. The review queue is not merely a safety net — it is the mechanism by which the system gets better. Every item an analyst clears is a labelled example, and the alias is the cheapest possible way of using one.

It is also the honest answer to "how does this improve over time?" without reaching for machine learning: the first improvement loop costs one table and one function.

Deployment shape

Three containers. Postgres, the API, and nginx serving the built React bundle and proxying /api to the backend — so the browser only ever talks to one origin and CORS never enters the picture in production. CORS is configured for development only, and locked to localhost rather than *, so it is not a habit that ships.

The upload endpoint takes CSV because that is what a demo can use. A production deployment receives CAMT.053 or MT940 from the bank, and swapping the parser is the whole of that change: everything downstream takes transaction rows and does not care where they came from.

It is deployed, on AWS in ap-south-1, entirely from Terraform — a t3.micro in a public subnet, RDS Postgres in private subnets with no route to the internet, images pulled from ECR, secrets from SSM Parameter Store, and no inbound SSH port at all. The architecture, the security decisions, the cost breakdown and the three things that broke on the way are a companion document: the deployment record.

Where the LLM sits — and where it does not

This is the distinction the project is built around, and a likely interview probe. The LLM reads unstructured text and proposes structure. It never decides anything, never produces a number that is trusted on its own, and is never required for the system to run.

  • Schema-constrained. Output must validate against a strict Pydantic model, or it is rejected.
  • One retry. Invalid output is retried once with the validation error fed back, then abandoned.
  • Cross-checked. Extracted per-invoice amounts must reconcile against the actual bank credit. If they do not, the extraction is distrusted rather than believed.
  • Optional. A regex extractor covers the common patterns, and a mock mode makes the entire test suite runnable with no API key and no network. The extraction method is recorded on every row, because an LLM result and a regex result do not deserve equal confidence.

12Design calls

Six rules the codebase enforces mechanically rather than by convention, because a rule that lives only in a code review is a rule that eventually breaks.

RuleHow it is enforced
Money is never a float rupees_to_paise() raises TypeError on a float argument rather than converting it. A test walks every column in the schema and fails if any _paise column is not an integer type, or if a Float column exists anywhere.
Everything is reproducible A single RANDOM_SEED drives all synthetic generation. The same seed produces byte-identical data, so an accuracy number from last week is comparable to one from today.
LLM output is never trusted Pydantic validation, one retry, amount cross-check, and a recorded extraction_method so downstream scoring can weight an LLM result differently from a regex one.
The LLM is optional LLM_MODE defaults to mock. The full test suite passes with no API key and no network.
Every decision is explainable explanation and reason_text are non-nullable columns on match_results, alongside engine_version so decisions from different builds stay comparable.
The matcher cannot see the answers Ground truth is a file on disk, not a table. A test asserts no scenario or truth column exists on bank_transactions.

Known limitations, stated up front

  • Synthetic data flatters the matcher. Generated messiness is messy in the ways I thought to model. Real bank files contain failure modes nobody anticipates, and the honest expectation is that measured accuracy drops on first contact with real data.
  • Scoring weights are hand-tuned, not learned. Defensible at this data volume and far more explainable, but a gradient-boosted model over the same signals would almost certainly score better once there is enough labelled review history to train on.
  • One currency. INR only. Multi-currency brings FX gain/loss on the application date, which is a genuine modelling problem, not a column.
  • No real bank formats yet. CSV upload only; CAMT.053 and MT940 are next steps, not current capability.

The 19 errors, and what they turned out to be

The first evaluation came back at 87.2% auto-match, 97.28% precision — 19 payments auto-applied to the wrong invoices, ₹2.65 crore misapplied. Then the breakdown:

costly errors: 19
  by scenario : {'short_pay_deduction': 19}
  by strategy : {'reference_exact': 16, 'remittance_guided': 3}

  RIGHT invoices, wrong amounts: 19
  wrong invoices entirely      :  0

Every single error was the same defect, and a narrow one: a short-pay across several invoices where the engine found the right invoice set and attributed the deduction to the wrong invoice within it. Not one error involved picking the wrong invoices.

The matcher's rule is "the smallest invoice that can absorb the gap". That is a reasonable prior and sometimes simply not what happened. When a remittance says "three invoices, ₹4,900 withheld" and never says which consignment was damaged, the attribution is a guess — and a guess posted at 96% confidence is exactly the failure this project exists to avoid.

The fix was not to tune the heuristic. Making the bearer rule match the generator would have scored beautifully and taught the system nothing that survives contact with a real client. It would be fitting to the synthetic data.

The fix was to stop pretending the guess is knowledge. A multi-invoice short-pay whose claim-bearing invoice nobody asserted now carries a penalty that drops it into the review queue, where two minutes of an analyst's time settles it. When the advice does name the invoice, the match is asserted and the penalty does not apply.

Result: 87.2% at 97.28% → 81.9% at 100%. Five points of automation traded for every error in the run.

What each signal is actually worth

Zero one signal's weight, redistribute it proportionally across the rest, re-score, re-judge. The drop is that signal's contribution — measured rather than asserted, which is the only way to defend a weighting in a room.

Precision stays at 100% in every variant. That is the finding, and it is a humbling one: none of the weights are currently load-bearing for correctness. They tune how much gets automated, not whether it is right.

Safety is coming from somewhere else entirely — the structural refusals. The ambiguity margin that declines to pick between two customers scoring alike, the penalty that sends a guessed claim-bearer to review, the cascade's refusal to match when the payer name and the reference disagree. Those are what produce 100% precision. The weighted sum decides volume.

That is worth knowing before anyone spends a week tuning weights to buy accuracy they will not get.

Two readings worth taking care over

  • amount_match contributes nothing measurable despite carrying 0.26 of the weight. The reason is structural: the cascade only produces a candidate when the amounts already work, so this signal is very nearly constant at 1.0 — and a signal that never varies carries no information. It is a candidate for re-weighting, though not on the evidence of one dataset.
  • Removing remittance_agreement slightly helps (+1.0 point). Advice that is present but does not support the match scores zero and drags the confidence down; removing the signal removes that drag. Arguably correct behaviour being measured as a cost — contradicting advice should lower confidence — but worth being honest that it costs automation.

One caveat on method: because removing a signal that scored 1.0 and redistributing its weight lowers any score that was not already perfect, this ablation measures how much each signal props up the confidence level, not how well it separates right from wrong. With precision pinned at 100% there is no separation left to measure, which is itself the answer.

Honest limitations of this result

  • 100% precision is a property of this dataset, not a promise. It means the engine makes no mistakes on messiness I thought to model. Real bank files contain failure modes nobody anticipates, and the first number to move on contact with real data is this one.
  • Deduction reason accuracy is 24% (18 of 75). Most claims stay unknown because only 196 of 800 payments have advice linked at all. A claim without a category cannot be routed, so this is the clearest gap with a known cause.
  • Amount-only matches never auto-apply — all 43 were correct here, and all 43 went to review. That is deliberate: these amounts are distinctive because they are synthetic. On a real book with round negotiated prices they would collide, and the caution would be earning its keep.
  • One payment per invoice. The generator never splits an invoice across two payments, so the two-instalment case is untested end to end.

One deliberate inconsistency

Rounding happens here, and nowhere else in the codebase. Every other number is an exact integer count of paise, enforced by tests that walk the whole schema and every API payload.

These are projections built from estimates, not ledger entries. The exactness rule exists to protect money that actually moved; applying it to a forecast would be precision theatre, and pretending a guess about analyst minutes is accurate to the paise would be its own kind of dishonesty.

16Interview Q&A

Questions this project invites, with the answer worth having ready.

Why rules rather than a machine-learning model?

Three reasons, in order of weight. First, there is no labelled training data on day one of a deployment — a rules engine works from the first payment, and the review queue it generates is the labelling process. Second, finance needs to audit decisions; “the model scored 0.87” does not survive a conversation with a controller, whereas “the invoice reference matched exactly and the amount tied to the paise” does. Third, thresholds are directly tunable — a client who wants fewer errors moves one number and sees the trade-off immediately.

The right end state is a hybrid: keep the deterministic signals as features, and learn the weights with something like LightGBM once there is enough review history. The architecture is already shaped for that — the signal breakdown is stored on every decision, so the training set builds itself.

What auto-match rate are you targeting, and why not 100%?

100% is the wrong target because it requires auto-applying cases the system is not confident about, and those are exactly the ones it gets wrong. The objective is the highest automation rate achievable while holding precision above the bar the client sets — typically very high, because a mis-applied payment costs far more to unwind than a reviewed one costs to clear.

So the deliverable is not a number, it is a curve. The threshold sweep shows what each cutoff buys in automation and costs in errors, and the client picks their point on it. A conservative controller and an aggressive shared-services lead will legitimately choose differently.

How do you stop the LLM inventing an amount?

Four layers, and the last one is the one that actually matters. The output must validate against a strict Pydantic schema. Invalid output is retried once with the error fed back. A regex extractor covers the common patterns if the LLM is unavailable or keeps failing.

And then the arithmetic check: extracted per-invoice amounts have to reconcile against the bank credit that actually arrived. A hallucinated figure almost never balances. If the numbers do not tie, the extraction is treated as a weak hint rather than a fact, and the payment goes to review instead of being auto-applied. The LLM gets to propose structure; it never gets to decide.

Why integer paise instead of Decimal?

Both are exact, so this is a performance and safety argument rather than a correctness one. Subset-sum evaluates many thousands of candidate combinations per payment, and integer addition is substantially faster than Decimal addition in that inner loop.

The safety half is that an integer cannot silently lose precision on the way through JSON, a CSV parse, or a careless division, whereas a Decimal can quietly become a float at any of those boundaries. Decimal is used in exactly one place — parsing a rupee string at the edge — and the result is immediately converted to paise. A test fails the build if any _paise column is not an integer type.

Subset-sum is NP-complete. How does this not blow up?

By never running it on a large set. Three prunes, applied before the search starts. Restricting candidates to the identified customer's open items cuts the pool from every invoice in the business to typically 10–60. A date window around the payment trims it further — an invoice from fourteen months ago is not what this payment settles. And capping the combination size at k bounds the work at C(n, k) instead of 2ⁿ.

Concretely: 40 candidate invoices with k = 4 is about 102,000 combinations, a few milliseconds. The unpruned power set is 1.1 × 10¹². The cost is that a genuine seven-invoice bundle will not be found — and that is the correct failure, because it goes to review rather than producing a confident wrong answer.

Two different invoice sets sum to exactly the payment amount. Now what?

Ambiguity is a signal, not an obstacle. When more than one candidate set ties on amount, confidence drops and the item goes to review with both options presented and the reason stated — “two combinations match this amount exactly; no reference was supplied to distinguish them.”

Picking one arbitrarily would be the worst available behaviour: a coin flip, posted with high apparent confidence, with nothing to show the reviewer later. The scoring model is designed so that ambiguity actively suppresses confidence rather than being ignored.

What about a customer who never sends remittance advice at all?

Common, and the system degrades rather than fails. Without advice it falls back to amount-based strategies against that customer's open items, and precision depends on how distinctive their invoice amounts are. A customer with four open invoices of very different values is still matched confidently; one with twenty near-identical monthly invoices mostly goes to review, which is the honest outcome.

The deployment answer is that this is a measurable, nameable gap: per-customer auto-match rates make it obvious which relationships would benefit from asking for structured advice. That conversation — using the data to change the client's process, not just to automate around it — is a good part of what a forward deployed role is for.

How do you know your accuracy numbers are real and not self-graded?

The ground truth is generated alongside the synthetic data and written to a separate file on disk. It is never loaded into the database the matcher queries, and a test asserts that no scenario, truth or expected column exists on bank_transactions — so the leak cannot be reintroduced by accident later.

The evaluation script is the only component that reads both the ground truth and the pipeline output, and it runs after matching is complete. The weakness I would flag unprompted is a different one: the data is synthetic, so it is messy in the ways I thought to model. Real bank files will contain failure modes I did not anticipate, and the number will drop.

How would this integrate with a real ERP?

Three touch points. Inbound master data — customers and open items — arrives by scheduled extract or API; the schema maps directly onto the open-items view that SAP, Oracle or NetSuite already expose. Inbound bank data arrives as CAMT.053 or MT940 from the bank rather than CSV; that is a parser swap behind the same interface, not a redesign.

Outbound is the one that needs care: posting the cash application back as a journal entry has to be idempotent and reversible, which is what statement_ref uniqueness and the audit log are already for. In practice the first deployment runs in suggest-only mode, posting nothing, until the client has watched the precision number for a few weeks.

What breaks first at ten times the volume?

Not the database — a few million rows with these indexes is unremarkable. The first pressure point is subset-sum on customers with large open-item counts, because C(n, k) grows fast in n; the mitigations are a tighter date window, a dynamic programming formulation over the paise amounts, or dropping to review earlier for high-n customers.

The second is LLM throughput and cost on remittance extraction, which is why extraction is batched, cached on the remittance row rather than recomputed, and skipped entirely when the regex extractor already produces a complete, balancing result.

Why is this a forward deployed engineering problem rather than a data science one?

Because almost none of the difficulty is in the algorithm. It is in learning that this particular distributor's largest customer pays by consolidated weekly transfer with advice in a spreadsheet attachment; that their deduction codes do not match the master list; that the AR team will not trust automation until they have watched it agree with them for a month.

The engineering that matters is the part that makes those discoveries cheap: a configurable scenario mix, tunable thresholds, an explanation on every decision, and an evaluation harness that can answer “did that change help?” in one command. The model is the easy half.
